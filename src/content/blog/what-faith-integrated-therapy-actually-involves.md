Faith-integrated therapy can mean very different things in different practices.

If faith matters deeply to you, it makes sense to want to know what that will actually look like in the room. Will it be treated as a source of meaning, a private matter, a symptom, or something too delicate to touch. The phrase itself does not answer that question.

So here is what it means in my work, including some of the parts that are not obvious from the label alone.

## What it does not mean

**It does not mean we pray in session.** Some practices do, and for the right person that can be meaningful. It is not the form my work takes. Therapy asks for a different kind of attention, and prayer within a therapeutic relationship can introduce an authority dynamic I would rather not bring into a room where we are often trying to understand exactly those dynamics.

**It does not mean I share your beliefs and will confirm them.** Reassurance can be comforting, but when it arrives too quickly, it can close down a question that needs more room. If you bring me something painful or confusing about your faith, my task is not to hand you back a ready-made frame. It is to help us stay close enough to the experience to understand what is happening there.

**It does not mean your faith is off limits for examination.** If faith is integrated, then it is part of the room and available to be looked at. That is different from treating it as fragile or beyond question. A therapist does not honor your faith by avoiding it. They honor it by being willing to take it seriously.

**And it does not mean the goal is deconstruction.** Some people carry that fear for good reason. I do not have a destination I am steering you toward. Therapy can make room for faith to remain central, to change, to become more complicated, or to feel unresolved for a long time.

## What it does mean

### Your religious history counts as history

In therapy, early life is usually understood as formative material: family, attachment, what felt safe, what it took to be acceptable, what love and authority came to mean. Faith-integrated work extends that same seriousness to the religious part of your life rather than bracketing it off.

For many people, that is a significant part of the story. If you grew up in church, some of your earliest ideas about love, obedience, worth, forgiveness, desire, shame, and belonging came bundled with it. Those ideas do not stay neatly contained in religion. They continue to shape marriage, work, family, conflict, and your relationship to your own wanting.

Treating that as history does not mean indicting it. It means becoming curious about what it formed.

### You do not have to translate

This is one of the first practical differences people notice.

If you tell me you still feel under conviction, or that the altar call is where things began to go sideways, or that you were the pastor's kid, or that you signed a purity pledge at fourteen, I know what world you are referring to. You do not have to spend half the session providing background or deciding how much of the strangeness of it to explain.

That matters partly because it saves time, but mostly because it changes what you are willing to say. People edit heavily when they expect they will have to justify their world to the listener. The edited version is rarely where the real material is.

### We separate things that got fused

This is much of the clinical work itself.

Religious formation can bind together things that are not the same: the voice of God and the voice of a particular adult, being loved and being compliant, doubt and betrayal, your body and danger, grief and ingratitude. These meanings often came together early and have continued operating as if they belong to one another.

That is one reason arguing with them rarely works. You cannot reason your way out of a fusion you cannot yet see. In therapy, we go slowly enough for some of those connections to become visible. The God you know yourself to believe in may feel different from the figure who appears in your body at two in the morning after you have made a mistake. As those begin to separate, something often becomes possible that was not before.

### Ambivalence is allowed to stay ambivalent

Many people arrive expecting to be moved toward a conclusion because so much of the surrounding conversation is organized around one: stay or go, believe or do not, commit or leave.

Some of the most meaningful work happens in people who do not quickly resolve those questions. They stop needing immediate closure. The unresolvedness becomes something they can carry without it costing them their sleep, their relationships, or their sense of themselves. That can be a deeply honest place rather than a failure to finish.

## What a session is like

Nothing dramatic. We talk. I ask about what has been on your mind or what feels important to begin with, and then we follow where the material leads.

Faith does not always arrive in the room in a direct way. Often it shows up sideways: in a conflict with your boss that carries an odd charge, in a reaction to your child's disobedience that feels larger than the situation, in a sentence you say and immediately qualify. Part of my work is to notice the qualifier and wonder with you about what it is doing there.

This is open-ended weekly work rather than a curriculum with a set number of sessions. Depth work needs enough continuity for patterns to show up in the room, which they eventually do. You can read more about [how I work](/approach/) if you want a fuller picture of the process, and about [what actually happens in a session](/blog/what-happens-in-a-depth-therapy-session/) if the mechanics are what you are wondering about.

## Why I can do this particular version of it

Before I trained clinically, I spent years studying formation, faith, and the inner life. I hold an M.A. in Spiritual Formation and Soul Care from Talbot School of Theology. I am also in postgraduate psychoanalytic training at the Newport Psychoanalytic Institute.

That background means I can take faith seriously as real and meaningful, while also being curious about how it has been formed, what it has cost, and what shape it has taken in your life. You do not need to spend your sessions translating theological language, and you do not need to worry that I will rush to reassure you out of the very question we need to understand.

## If you are trying to decide whether this fits

A helpful question to ask a prospective therapist is not simply whether they respect faith. It is whether they can hold your faith as real and hold what it has cost you as real, at the same time, without needing to resolve the tension for their own comfort.
