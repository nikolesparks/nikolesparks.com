You sat down on Tuesday and nothing came, and you told yourself it was a bad day, because everyone has those. Wednesday went the same way and you blamed the sleep. By the second week you'd stopped explaining it to yourself and started going around it instead, answering email, taking the meeting, reorganizing the files, doing all the parts of the job that are still possible, and the file you actually care about has been open in a tab since March. You haven't told anyone. You're not sure what you'd say.

Most creative people arrive at one word for this, and the word is lazy. I'd want to look at almost anything else first. In the work I do a block like that reads less like a failure of discipline than like a part of you refusing to go on with an arrangement, and the arrangement usually goes back a long way.

From the outside nothing looks wrong, which is a good part of why it's lonely. You're still delivering. The client work goes out and the invoices go out and people say kind things about the last project and mean them, and you take the compliments the way you'd take them on behalf of somebody who used to live here. What's missing is the part that was yours. And because the visible output hasn't dropped, nobody asks, and you don't raise it, because raising it would mean saying out loud that you're frightened about something that isn't technically happening.

## When the work became the person

If you were the kid who could draw, or write, or play, or make things, there's a decent chance that's how you got seen.

It made you legible at school, where being good at something is a way of being placed. It may have made you valuable at home. In a house where attention was scarce or unreliable, having a thing you were known for is a position, and a position is something you hold onto. And so the making stopped being something you did and became something closer to who you were, which happened gradually and early and without anybody deciding it, least of all you.

That arrangement produces good work. It also produces a particular kind of exposure, because if you are what you make, then a stretch of not making isn't a quiet season, it's a question about whether you're still the person you've been telling everyone you are. The question is intolerable to sit inside. So you push, and the pushing tends to go badly, and the bad result confirms the fear, and then you push again from a worse position.

Winnicott had an idea about this that I keep coming back to. He thought creativity lives in play, and that play requires a space where nothing is at stake, where you can mess about without anybody grading it, including yourself. When every hour of making has become an audition, that space closes. What's left is production, which is a different activity entirely and considerably less generative, and which can be sustained for years by people who are good at effort.

## Everything you've already tried

By the time somebody calls me about this they've usually done a lot.

A new schedule, a different desk, morning pages, an accountability partner, deadlines they set themselves and deadlines they asked somebody else to hold, a residency, a sabbatical, one more app. None of that was foolish. Most of it was sensible, and some of it worked for a while, which is what makes the next part so demoralizing: when the block comes back after a period of it going well, it now looks like evidence. You could do it. You stopped. The case against you gets stronger every time you prove you're capable.

I'd read the return of the block differently, and you're free to disagree. If making has become the main place where you get evaluated, and the evaluation never quite finishes, then the part of you that stopped may not be broken at all. It may be applying the brakes on something it has decided is no longer safe.

That's not a comfortable idea, and I don't hand it to people as a conclusion. It's a direction to look in, and most people who look find something there.

## Underneath the block

What turns up varies, and a few things turn up a lot.

Sometimes the work stopped being yours somewhere along the way. It began as the thing you did because you couldn't not do it, and then it became the thing you deliver, and position, and defend in meetings, and post about, and price. Nobody makes that trade on purpose. It happens one reasonable decision at a time, and then one day you're looking at a project you chose and feeling nothing about it.

Sometimes something else has been waiting underneath. A grief nobody made room for. Anger at a collaborator or a client or a parent that never had anywhere to go. A decision about a relationship that you've been not making for two years. The block can function as a container for all of that, and the stopping is easier to look at than the thing that stopped you.

Sometimes it was the last good thing that did it. Something landed, and it was received well, and rather than loosening the grip it tightened it, because now there's a standard in the room with a number attached to it and whatever comes next has to clear that number. Success is an odd sort of pressure. People aren't very sympathetic about it, understandably, which means most of the ones carrying it carry it on their own.

And sometimes the pace was never survivable and the body has called it. That explanation gets dismissed because it's unglamorous, and it's true often enough that I always want to ask about the last four years before we go looking for anything deeper.

## The audience that moved in

There's usually somebody watching.

Not literally, and not a person you could name in most cases, though sometimes you can: a teacher, a first editor, a parent who was proud of you in a way that felt conditional and wasn't always available. Whoever it was, the watching didn't stop when they left. It got installed. Now you sit down to work and there's an evaluation running before the first mark goes down, and the evaluation is in a voice that sounds like your own judgment and mostly isn't.

Adam Phillips writes about how much of a life gets organized around an ideal version of ourselves that we can never meet and never quite give up on either, and about how punishing that arrangement is, and how attached to it we stay. Creative people tend to be extremely attached. The ideal is what made the early work good. It's also what's currently sitting in the chair across from you every morning with its arms folded.

One of the stranger effects is what happens to praise. Somebody tells you the work is good, and you can hear the sentence, and you can tell they mean it, and it doesn't go in anywhere. It gets filed instantly with all the other compliments from people who don't know what you cut, or how long it took, or what you were going for and missed. The figure doing the actual grading isn't in the room, and it doesn't accept outside evidence.

Some of the work is getting acquainted with that figure. Where it came from, whose voice it uses, what it was protecting you from back when its standards were the price of being loved.

## Rest that never counts as rest

Ask most people in this position when they last rested and they'll tell you about a vacation.

Then ask what they did on it, and there's a laptop in the story somewhere, or a notebook, or a museum visited mainly for research, or three days of being ill because the body waited for permission. Time off isn't the same as rest. Rest requires that you're not on the hook, and if you've been on the hook since you were eleven, a week in another country doesn't change your status.

So the guilt follows you there. You lie on a beach and feel the low insistence that you should be using this, and you either give in and start using it, or you don't and the not-using becomes its own small torment. Either way the week doesn't do what weeks are supposed to do, and you come home tired, and conclude that rest doesn't work on you.

You can say all of that out loud without making a case for it first. It doesn't need to be justified by how hard you've been working.

## Where we'd start

I don't come at this by trying to get you producing again.

That's not a knock on wanting to produce; you probably do want to, and there's nothing wrong with wanting it. But if output and worth have grown into each other, then aiming straight at output leaves the knot exactly where it is and pulls it slightly tighter. What we'd be after instead is a self that survives a bad month, which is slower to build and holds up better.

Practically that means asking where the terms got set, and who set them, and what happens in you when you picture a whole month of making nothing at all. It means looking at what the pause threatens to expose, because it's usually threatening to expose something. And it means paying attention to how all of this shows up between us, which it will: you'll want to be an interesting client, you'll arrive with the week shaped into a narrative, you'll notice yourself checking whether the hour was productive. When that happens we've got the pattern live in the room, which beats reconstructing it afterward.

A note on how it tends to go early. People come in and talk about the work, the project, the deadline, the client who changed the brief three times, and that's fine, because that's usually where the material is sitting. Then somewhere around the fourth session the conversation slides off the project and onto a Sunday afternoon when you were nine, or something your father said about a drawing, and you apologize for going off topic. You won't be off topic. That's the same subject arriving from a different direction, and it tends to arrive on its own once there's somewhere for it to land.

Plenty of people find the work comes back as this goes on, and I'd rather describe that than promise it. The change most people name later is a different one anyway: that a stretch of not making stopped feeling like a referendum.

It's open-ended. Some people stay a season, some stay considerably longer, and nobody gets moved along on a timetable.

## If you recognize this

The word lazy does a lot of damage inside people who are exhausted, or frightened, or so bound up with what they make that stopping feels like vanishing. It's also a remarkably poor description of somebody who has spent six months trying every available method to get themselves back to a desk.

You don't need to arrive with any of it worked out, and you don't need to have produced something recently to have a right to the hour. Coming in during the empty stretch is arguably better, since the empty stretch is the thing we'd be looking at.
