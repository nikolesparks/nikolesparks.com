What most people want to know before they reach out isn't really the logistics. It's something more like: what will be expected of me, and will I be able to do it.

You can't be bad at this. I know that's a flimsy answer on its own, so here's the longer one.

One thing to say from the get-go: nothing about our time together is meant to fool you. There's no correct way to use the time, no quota of feeling you're supposed to reach, and no version of this where you turn up and fail at therapy. Whatever you bring, including the weeks where you aren't sure what to bring, is the material we work with.

## The first few minutes

You come in, you sit down, and I ask something open. Usually where you'd like to start, or what's been on your mind.

Some people arrive with our time together already shaped: something happened on Thursday, they've been turning it over since, they want to get into it straight away. Other people come in with nothing in particular and say so, usually a little apologetically, as though they've turned up to their hair stylist without washing their hair. That's fine, and it's actually more than fine. What you reach for when you haven't planned anything tends to be significant and rich, and some of the better hours I've been part of started with somebody saying they didn't have anything today.

There's no ritual to get through at the start and no fixed structure to the hour. That's deliberate. An agenda set in advance would decide what we're going to look at before either of us has arrived, and that type of deciding is usually done by the same part of you that has been keeping certain things out of view for awhile.

The first session has a bit more shape than the ones after it. I'll want to know what brought you in, and why now, as opposed to last year or next year, because timing is usually revealing. I'll ask some practical things about your life and your history. And at some point I'll ask what you're hoping for, and if your answer is that you don't know, that's a perfectly good answer we can start from and work together to put language to.

If you're seeing me by video rather than in person, most of this is the same. The rhythm of the hour runs pretty much the same way. What changes is small and practical: you'll want to be somewhere you won't be overheard, which for a lot of people ends up being in their parked car, which ends up being a great pseudo therapy office. Some people find it easier to say hard things from their living room while the kids are down. Others their actual home office.

## The middle, the working through

The bulk of the hour is the two of us talking, and me following where you go.

I'm listening to several things at once: what you're saying, and how you're saying it, and where you steer away from, and what sounds familiar but transposed, and what shifts in the room when you get near something tender, and the sentence you take back half a second after it leaves your mouth. 

So, every so often I'll ask about something that seemed small. You mention your sister called, and then you move on to the work situation you came in to talk about, and I'll come back to your sister. Something happened in your voice when you said it. I'd like to stay there a minute.

You'll notice I don't rush to hand over a solution or something to do. From my vantage point, you've usually already tried the sensible things, the things you can google. You've read, and thought about it carefully, and probably given the same good advice to a friend. The problem you're bringing me is almost never a problem of what to do. It's usually much more complex, the problem where you know what to do and find yourself doing the other thing, or you actually have no clue what to do because you're divided against yourself and aren't sure which version to listen to, or you've been so out of contact with what you want you truly aren't sure. In each of these the solution is not adding more instructions to the pile; it's understanding what the block is and freeing you to choose with more clarity.

## The past arrives on its own time in its own way

This isn't the version people picture, a version where we begin in your childhood and work forward through the decades in order.

Usually, the past comes up by itself, because it's still running in the present. You'll be describing some friction with your boss, some way you went silent in a meeting when you had every right to speak, and there'll be a familiarity in how you handled it that neither of us can place at first. So we explore it. And more times than not it belongs somewhere much earlier, in a house where going silent was the intelligent move, and once that comes into view the thing with your boss looks and feels different.

Understanding it helps. But what shifts things is catching the pattern while it's happening, in real time, with somebody else there. A small amount of room opens between the moment and the response, where before there was none at all.

## The relationship itself

The part people are least prepared for is that our relationship becomes material too.

You'll have reactions to me. You might notice you want my approval, or that you brace slightly before saying something, or that you got irritated by something small I said in week six and have been carrying it since. You might feel like I could be doing more, or you might leave a session certain you talked too much. None of this is static around the work. It's important info you bring with you everywhere else, and arriving somewhere it can finally be looked at while it's live, rather than explained away or reconstructed afterward from a story.

It's why I'll sometimes ask what it was like to say a particular thing to me. I'm not doing it to put you on the spot. Your answer is usually incredibly clear emotional information we don't always get in the hour.

It also means that if you want to say something uncomfortable about the therapy itself, to my face, I want you to. Most of us learned early to protect the person we come to for help from our honest reactions, and to do it so smoothly that we don't notice we're doing it. Here, your reactions and feelings toward me are welcome, important, and invited.

## Silence

There'll be some. More than some.

Sometimes a silence means you're thinking and I'd rather not walk across it. Sometimes something has landed and needs a moment to breathe before it can be spoken about. And sometimes it's just uncomfortable, which is its own material, because for a lot of people quiet with another person never felt neutral. Silence in a house where somebody was angry or distant and unreachable is of a different substance than silence in a house where people were relaxed and easy with each other.

If it ever feels like too much, say so. That's a good thing to bring into the room, and we can look at it together.

## The end

Endings have a character of their own.

People often say important things at the end of the hour or with four minutes left. That's not an issue of timekeeping; it's what happens when something has been getting closer and closer for fifty minutes and the end of the session finally gives it a safe container to arrive in. I'll usually name it and we'll pick it up next week.

You may also find the hour doesn't wrap up neatly. Depth work doesn't tie off each session with a conclusion, and some weeks you'll leave stirred up rather than settled. I say that in advance so it doesn't get read as a sign something's going wrong. A lot of the movement happens in the days between, in a dream, on a drive or in the shower, with no particular warning.

## How long people stay

The work is open-ended.

The patterns we're after don't surface on demand. They come out once there's been enough rhythm and steadiness between two people for them to risk it, and how long that takes differs from person to person; the mind has its own time and it needs to be respected. Typically we'll meet weekly as our usual rhythm. This is because the work builds on itself and the continuity holds the tough stretches and provides enough consistency to let it build.

People sometimes want to stop and feel strange about talking about it, as though leaving would be a criticism of the work, or me. It isn't, and saying it out loud tends to feel better than handling it by cancelling twice and then disappearing. Endings that get talked about tend to be useful in their own right, and a fair number of people who bring it up end up staying a bit longer, having found something important in the bringing up.

Some people stay a year. Some stay several. Others come for a defined season, around something specific, and stop when it feels contained. There's no grade at ending, and nowhere in particular you have to end up for the time to have been meaningful.

## Who this tends to fit

Psychodynamic therapy is for people who can sense something is off, there is a pattern, and they've tried to do something about it, and it keeps happening anyway.

That gap between seeing and changing is what depth work is made for. This is partly why the people who tend to come in are usually thoughtful, creative, and capable, and a few rounds into thinking on the problem already. They're not usually looking for a downloadable; they're looking for someone to help them see something they can't see about what's happening. They're tired of feeling this thing continuing to happen. They want more choice and they need help.

Couples work runs on the same logic with two people in the room. The subject is usually one or more recurring argument, and the work is slowing it down enough to see what each of you was responding to, which is rarely what is actually getting said.

You don't need to arrive with any of this articulated. You can come in and say that something isn't right and you can't name it, and we'll find the language together. That's a very respectable and human way to begin.

## If you're considering it

The thing I hear most from people a few months in is that they didn't expect to end up talking about what they ended up talking about.
