If you're wondering what a session is actually like before you book one, that's a fair thing to want to know. Most people are asking something more specific than the words let on.

You might be wondering whether there will be long silences you're expected to fill. Whether you're supposed to arrive with a topic. Whether I'll be quietly sizing you up the whole time, or whether it feels like a conversation or something more clinical. And underneath a lot of it, gently: whether you'll somehow be bad at this.

You can't really be bad at it, and I mean that. It isn't a satisfying answer on its own, so let me walk you through how it goes.

## The first few minutes

You come in, you settle into a chair, and I ask something open. Usually just where you'd like to start, or what's been on your mind.

Some people arrive with an agenda. Something happened this week, they've been turning it over, they want to get into it. Others come in with nothing in particular and say so, sometimes a little apologetically, and that's completely fine. What you reach for when you haven't planned anything is frequently the most honest way in.

There's no ritual to get through at the start, and no set structure. None of that is an accident. A fixed agenda would decide what we look at before we'd arrived, and deciding ahead of time is part of what keeps the important things out of view.

## The middle, which is most of it

Most of the hour is the two of us talking, and me following where you go.

That can sound passive. It isn't. I'm listening to several things at once: what you're saying, how you're saying it, where you steer away from, what changes in the room when you get near something tender, the thing you take back a second after it's out of your mouth. The last two carry a lot.

So now and then I'll ask about something that seems small. You mention your sister called, then move on to the work thing you came in to talk about, and I'll circle back to your sister. Something happened in your voice when you said it, and it's worth staying with for a moment.

You'll notice I don't rush to hand you a solution. If you describe a situation and pause, half expecting a strategy, sometimes what you get back is a question about the pause. I'm not holding out on you. You've almost certainly arrived with plenty of strategies already, and if the right tip were the missing piece, you probably wouldn't have needed to come.

## The past, when it comes up

This isn't the version people picture, where we start in your childhood and work forward in order.

The past comes up on its own, because it's still alive in the present. You'll be describing some friction with your boss, and there's a familiarity in how you responded that neither of us can place at first. So we stay with it. More often than not, it belongs somewhere earlier, and once that comes into view the situation with your boss looks different, because you're no longer the only moving part in it.

The insight matters, and on its own it helps less than people hope. What moves things is catching a pattern while it's actually happening: a small bit of room opens where an automatic reaction used to be. It widens slowly.

## The room itself

The part people are least ready for is that our relationship becomes part of the material too.

You'll have reactions to me. You might catch yourself wanting my approval, bracing a little for judgment, getting quietly annoyed by something small, or leaving a session sure you talked too much. None of that is noise around the work; it's the same patterns you carry everywhere else, showing up somewhere we can finally look at them together, in the moment, instead of reassembling them from a story afterward.

It's why I'll sometimes ask what it was like to say a particular thing to me. I'm not doing it to catch you out. Your answer is frequently the most direct information in the room.

It also means that if you want to say something uncomfortable about the therapy itself, to me, you can. Most of us learned early to shield the person with the authority from our honest reactions. Noticing that habit while it's happening is exactly the kind of thing we're here for.

## Silence

There will be some. It isn't a test.

Sometimes silence means you're thinking, and I'd rather not step on it. Sometimes something has landed and needs a moment to settle. And sometimes it's just uncomfortable, and even that is worth being curious about together, because for a lot of people silence with another person never felt neutral growing up.

If it ever feels like too much, say so. It's a fair thing to bring into the room, and we'll look at it together.

## No fixed number of sessions

Depth work doesn't come in a set number of sessions, and I don't offer it that way, because the patterns we're after don't appear on a schedule. They surface once there's been enough steadiness between us, and enough safety, for them to risk coming out. That timeline looks a little different for everyone.

Weekly is the usual rhythm. When sessions are spaced further apart, it's hard for anything to build, and more of each hour goes to catching up on the week than to the work underneath.

Some people stay with this for a year. Some for several. Others come in for a defined season, around something specific, and stop when it's shifted. There's no failing at stopping, and no rule that you have to end up anywhere in particular for it to have been worth your time.

## Who this is for

There's no homework, no worksheets, and no sequence of skills I move you through.

What this is built for is the spot where you already understand the pattern, and you've already tried to change it on purpose, and it keeps happening anyway. That gap between knowing and doing is the thing depth work is made to meet. It's part of why so many of the people I sit with are thoughtful, capable, and already several rounds into working on themselves.
