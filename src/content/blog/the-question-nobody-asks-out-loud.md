People will ask a prospective therapist about rates, availability, parking, and whether they take insurance.

Often they will not ask the thing they most want to know, which is: what are you going to do with my faith.

The reason is understandable. If you ask a therapist whether they respect religion, you will almost certainly hear yes. The question feels vulnerable, and the answer often does not tell you much. So instead people search, read a few pages, try to infer the answer, and either book someone and hope for the best, or wait longer than they want to.

A lot of people wait.

## What the search can actually turn up

When people look for a therapist using faith-related terms, they often find practices that emphasize the sustaining role of faith and others that focus more explicitly on religious harm and recovery. Both can be valuable. The real question is whether the clinician can meet the particular complexity you are bringing: what mattered, what hurt, what remains unresolved, and what you want to understand more clearly.

Some people are looking for support in a life where faith remains central. Others are trying to name injuries that happened within religious settings. Many people are somewhere between those descriptions, still connected in some ways, deeply troubled in others, unsure what they believe now, or unwilling to force a conclusion before they are ready.

If your experience sits in that middle space, it can be hard to know from a website whether a therapist will actually understand it.

## What to ask instead

Since "do you respect faith" will almost always get a reassuring answer, it helps to ask questions where the response gives you more information.

**"What do you do when a client's religious beliefs seem to be contributing to the problem?"**

Listen for whether the answer describes a process or just offers reassurance. A therapist who has done this kind of work will usually talk about getting curious, slowing down, distinguishing between what was taught and what was received, and making space for complexity. A therapist who has not may either promise never to challenge anything or immediately tell you what they think about the beliefs themselves.

**"Have you worked with people who stayed in their faith and people who left?"**

You are listening for whether the therapist has seen more than one outcome. It is not that one answer is always better. It is that you want to know whether the person you are considering can tolerate more than one direction.

**"What is your background with this kind of material?"**

Therapists vary in how much they will share, and that is legitimate. But it is often possible to tell whether the world you are describing feels familiar to them or whether you will need to spend the session translating it.

**And notice how they respond to being asked.**

This is one of the clearest signals available. Does the question seem reasonable to them, or do they become noticeably careful? Someone who is comfortable with this material will usually understand immediately why you are asking.

## What to notice on their website

Before the call, look for whether the practice can make room for more than one kind of relationship to faith.

A site that speaks about faith only as a strength may not be showing you how it understands pain, doubt, or injury. A site that speaks only about harm may not be speaking to someone who still wants to remain connected to what has mattered to them. What you are looking for is not perfection. It is some evidence that the therapist can make room for both what faith has meant and what it may have cost.

That is often the clearest signal that your situation will not need to resolve in a particular direction in order for the therapist to stay comfortable.

## Why neutrality matters more here

In many areas of therapy, a clinician having a private opinion about your choices is not ideal but is often survivable. Religion is different because the material is so often bound up with authority in the first place.

Much of what may need to be examined has to do with how you learned to defer, who was allowed to define what was true about you, and what happened when you disagreed with someone holding that kind of authority. If your therapist has a preferred outcome for your faith, they can quietly step into the same position the original trouble came from. And without meaning to, you may begin doing what you learned long ago to do in those situations: reading the room and offering the version of yourself that seems safest there.

That is not dishonesty. It is a well-practiced way of staying connected.

## How I try to hold this

I do not have an outcome I want for you. Some people I work with come through with their faith intact and different. Some come through without it. Some remain unresolved for a long time and find that the unresolvedness becomes more livable and less costly.

Part of why I can hold that is my background: an M.A. in Spiritual Formation and Soul Care from Talbot School of Theology, along with postgraduate psychoanalytic training at the Newport Psychoanalytic Institute. The theological world is not foreign to me, so you will not spend sessions explaining your language. The depth training helps us stay curious about what those experiences formed, without deciding too quickly what they should mean.
