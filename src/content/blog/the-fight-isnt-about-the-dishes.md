Most couples have one fight they keep having. The dishes. The tone. Being late. How long it takes to answer a text. The subject varies and the shape doesn't: same opening move, same middle, same ending, and you both know how it goes before it starts.

That it keeps coming back is the useful part. A disagreement that's actually about dishes gets solved by a schedule. One that returns after the schedule was already working is about something else, and until that something else gets named, each of you keeps concluding that the other isn't really trying.

## Underneath the subject

The surface issue moves around. What sits under it is steadier, and it usually comes down to a few questions nobody says out loud.

Do I matter to you. It rarely sounds like that. It sounds like a complaint about the phone at dinner, or about which family gets the holiday, and underneath is a question about priority and whether the relationship still carries weight.

Am I going to be left alone with this. Arguments about chores and logistics are frequently arguments about whether the partnership is actually shared.

Is it safe to need something from you. Some people learned early that needing was dangerous, or humiliating, or expensive. In adulthood the need comes out sideways: as criticism, as distance, as resentment.

Am I about to be made the problem. One partner hears any complaint as a verdict on their character, because that's how conflict worked where they grew up. So they defend themselves quickly, and the defending reads as indifference, and the complaint gets sharper.

Neither of you is only responding to what's being said. You're both responding to what it means, and those meanings were assigned long before you met.

## The loop

Couples don't usually get stuck for lack of communication advice.

Plenty of stuck couples are thoughtful and articulate and already know the vocabulary. What holds them is that each person's move sets off the other's protection. One reaches. The other pulls back to keep things from escalating, and the pulling back feels like abandonment, so the reaching gets more urgent, and the pulling back goes deeper.

Both moves made sense somewhere earlier in life. Together they make a loop neither of you would have built alone, and neither of you can interrupt alone.

That's why "we just need to communicate better" tends to fall flat. The words are arriving fine. They're landing on older injuries and getting read there.

## Slowing it down

In couples therapy we take the fight apart in slow motion.

That's more of the work than people expect. We take one recent example, usually something small from the past week, and move through it slowly enough to see what happened inside each of you at every turn. At full speed it's nearly invisible.

There's almost always a moment early on where something shifted before anything difficult had actually been said. A tone landed a certain way. A pause got interpreted. That moment matters more than the surface topic, because it's where the old material came into the room.

From there we get curious about where each response came from. Not to assign blame, and not to explain anyone away. But knowing that your partner's shutdown is an old protection rather than a statement about your worth changes what becomes possible next.

Over time the fight stops taking the same shape. The disagreement may still be there. What changes is that it stops carrying every previous version of itself, and becomes something the two of you can actually finish.

## Before you start

Couples therapy isn't two individual therapies in one room, and it doesn't require both people to be equally enthusiastic about being there. A lot of good work starts with one partner who wanted this and one who agreed to come. It also doesn't require you to know what's wrong. Most couples arrive with the surface version and find the real subject on the way.
