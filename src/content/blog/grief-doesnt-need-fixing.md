It's been long enough now that most people have stopped bringing it up. The ones who still do ask a little differently than they used to—with a hopefulness in it—and you can hear the hopefulness, so you tell them you're doing better. Sometimes that's true. Sometimes you say it because the other answer takes twenty minutes and you're standing in somebody's kitchen holding a drink. Either way you've got a version ready now, and within that version there's a count running: how long it's been, and whether that's too long.

Grief doesn't work on a schedule. There's no point on the calendar where it's supposed to be over. Being further out than you expected isn't evidence that something's gone wrong with you. When something is wrong, the question that helps isn't how many months it's been; it's whether the grief has some motion to it.

## Everyone goes back to their lives

For the first few weeks there are people around. Food shows up. Your phone doesn't stop. Somebody you hardly know says something to you in a parking lot that you think about for a week.

And you go back to work, usually sooner than you feel ready for, because the leave runs out. The first morning back is strange. The coffee machine is the same, your inbox is the same, somebody needs a decision by noon. People are careful with you for about a week. Then, the carefulness stops. You're a normal colleague again, which isn't an unreasonable thing to expect of you, and you meet it. Yet, meeting it every day costs something.

Then it thins out at home too, which it has to. People have their own lives and they go back to them. Most of us get very little practice at staying near someone who is still in process. The feelings are unfinished, as if they will ever be "finished," and after a while people run out of things to offer, so they offer hope instead. You seem better. You're doing so well. Time helps.

For the person who's lost something, the pressure you're feeling usually isn't coming from the grief. It's coming from the room around it.

Darian Leader has written about how much of mourning depends on other people being there for it: a witness, a ritual, some shared form that gives the loss a place to sit. When that thins out early, you're left carrying something in private that wasn't built to be private. By six months, a lot of people are doing exactly that, and it's tiring.

## When there's nowhere to put it

Grief that gets hurried doesn't finish sooner. It sits and waits.

It comes back later, and not always as sadness. As irritation that's out of proportion to whatever set it off. As a flatness. Maybe not depression, but it isn't quite living either. As anxiety with nothing attached to it, or a sudden inability to sit in a room where a particular song is on. Sometimes it turns up years later fastened to a much smaller loss, a pet or a move or somebody else's bad news, and the size of what you feel makes no sense next to the size of what happened.

There's a pattern of this that can run on the calendar. You hit a bad stretch in February, heavy and short with people and no idea why, and then you look at the date. That happens to a lot of people. The body tends to get there before the thinking does.

None of it means something has gone wrong with you. A feeling that needed more room was sent the message there wasn't any—so it waited.

## Anger, numbness, and relief

The picture most of us grow up with is a sad one, and sadness is in there. So is a good deal else.

Anger, for a start. At the doctors, at whoever handled the arrangements badly, at the person who died (this is the one nobody warns you about), at yourself for still caring so much. Then, numbness, which can go on for weeks and feel like evidence that you didn't love enough. Boredom, oddly. Long flat stretches where you feel close to nothing, and then ten minutes in the car that take everything you have.

And relief. Relief after a long illness, after a hard marriage, after years of being the one who handled it, and it tends to arrive with guilt attached, because it seems to say something unforgivable about how you felt. It doesn't. Relief after a long illness is a statement about the illness. Relief after a difficult relationship is a statement about the difficulty. Neither one is an indictment on the love.

There's also what happens on a good day. You get most of the way through an ordinary Saturday before you notice you haven't thought about them since morning, and then something turns over in your chest. People read that as forgetting, or as evidence they cared less than they believed they did. It's usually neither, and noticing it doesn't put you under any obligation to correct it.

Melanie Klein described mourning as work rather than a mood, and she was interested in what happens when the feelings run both ways at the same time. If you loved someone and were also furious with them, or needed them and couldn't be near them, both of those have to come through the loss with you. That takes longer. It's also much harder to say, because the public version of mourning has less room in it for the angry part.

You can say the angry part here. And the relieved part.

## Grieving something that's still alive

Not every loss comes with a service and a week off work.

Estrangement is a loss. Dementia is a long one, where the person is in the room and also isn't, and it arrives in pieces over years without a single day anyone can point to. Addiction. Divorce. A friendship that mattered enormously for ten years and then faded out, with neither of you doing anything "wrong."

And there's grief for something that never showed up at all. People who grew up with a difficult parent sometimes find, well into adulthood, that they're mourning two things: the parent, and the relationship they kept expecting would eventually start. 

There are also losses that are hard to talk about at all. A pregnancy. Somebody nobody knew you were close to. A person whose place in your life you didn't share with many people, so there's no one now who understands what went missing. Having no public word for a loss doesn't make it a smaller one.

These are harder partly because nobody around you treats them as losses. The grief is the same, but the support that comes automatically with other losses doesn't come at all.

## Whether it's been too long

People ask me this, usually carefully, usually near the end of the hour.

Time isn't the measure I'd use. Grief that's moving can hurt as much as it did in the second month and still be moving: what sets it off changes, what you can say about it changes, how much of the day it takes up changes. Grief that's stuck stays in the same position, month after month, without any of that shifting.

When it's stuck, something is usually holding it there. Anger you can't say because the person is gone and saying it feels like a betrayal. Guilt about a decision made in a hallway at two in the morning. Something that never got said, or got said badly the last time you spoke. A relationship you can't describe with all the nuance you hold inside to the people around you, because the honest description doesn't match what they think it was.

There's another measure people run privately, and it's tougher on them than the calendar. You compare. Your brother seems to be through it. A friend lost her mother the same year and was back to herself by spring. You take that as information about you, when it's information about two different relationships and what each one held.

Some grief does get complicated, and that's a reason to bring it somewhere rather than wait longer. Time on its own doesn't always soften something that hasn't had room to be felt all the way through.

## When faith was the thing holding it

For some people, a death also takes out the structure that was supposed to make death bearable.

If your faith gave you a way to hold this, and it didn't hold, you may be carrying two losses at once. There's the person. And there's the account of things that was supposed to steady you and isn't steadying you now, which can be hard to raise with people in your community, because saying it out loud can invite anxiety. So it stays unsaid, sitting next to the first loss, sometimes for a very long time. There is more on that particular double loss in [when you still believe and something still hurts](/blog/when-you-still-believe-and-something-still-hurts/).

## Coming in with it

There's no route here that runs from where you are to somewhere better on an agreed schedule.

What there can be is an hour a week where nobody's hoping you'll be further along than you are. Where the anger and the relief and the flat parts come out in whatever order they arrive, at whatever length they take, and nobody needs to steer you. I'm not counting the months either.

Most of what happens is slow and less dramatic than people expect. You tell me about it again, and it comes out a little differently than it did last time, and we notice the difference. Something you'd been reporting as a plain fact turns out, on the eighth telling, to have a lot of feeling in it. Klein's term for what's going on underneath is reinstatement: the slow business of getting the person settled inside you in a form you can keep. That isn't the same as letting them go, and it takes as long as it takes.

Some of it turns out to be less about the person than about what the loss rearranged. Who you are in your family now. A role that landed on you in the first month and never got discussed. Decisions made while you were in no condition to be making them, that you're living with now. Those tend to surface later, once the sharpest part has eased, and they're a part of the loss.

It's open-ended. Some people stay a season. Some stay a good deal longer. It ends when it's finished.

## Before you call

If you're running the count again while you read this, the one about whether you should be past this by now, you can put it down for a minute.

You don't need your thoughts organized first. You don't need a clear account of what the person was to you, and you don't need to work out ahead of time which parts are acceptable to say. Whatever's in there, including the parts you think reflect poorly on you, is welcome.
