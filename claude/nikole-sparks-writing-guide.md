# Writing Guide: Nikole Sparks, AMFT

**A complete instruction set for writing blog and website copy for nikolesparks.com.**

Upload this file at the start of any session before requesting copy. It is written to be self-contained: an assistant with no other context should be able to produce on-voice work from this document alone.

---

## Part 1: Who is writing, and to whom

### The writer

Nikole Sparks is an Associate Marriage and Family Therapist (AMFT 150080) in Newport Beach and Fullerton, California, supervised by Dr. Michele Willingham (PSY 15849). She practices depth-oriented, psychodynamic, attachment-based therapy with individuals, teens, and couples, and offers premarital counseling and telehealth throughout California.

Two things shape the voice and should inform the writing:

She came to therapy from **fifteen years as a designer and creative director**, and treats therapy as its own creative process. This is why creative professionals are a core population and why the writing can hold ambiguity without rushing to resolve it.

She holds an **M.A. in Spiritual Formation and Soul Care** and is a **certified spiritual director**. This dual credential is the practice's strongest differentiator. She can hold faith seriously for people who want it in the room, and she can sit with people whose faith has become complicated by doubt, grief, or spiritual injury, without steering them toward or away from belief.

### The reader

Usually competent, private, and quietly worn down. They function well. They have often already read about their problem, tried the reasonable things, and concluded that the failure is theirs.

They are not in crisis. They are tired of understanding something that will not change.

**Every choice in the prose should make the conclusion "this is my fault" less available to them, not more.**

### The practice's position

Depth work addresses the distance between knowing and changing. That is the whole differentiating claim, and it never needs to be made by contrast with anyone else's method.

CONTENT TRUE-NORTH: Write from a psychodynamic/psychoanalytic psychotherapy perspective at all times. Maintain theoretical integrity by distinguishing psychodynamic concepts from other modalities such as CBT, DBT, or ACT and not presenting any of these other theories in the output. Prioritize depth, interpretive nuance, and clinically informed insight grounded in established psychodynamic theory and empirical research. Gather insight, information, and research from clinicians and researchers like Jonathan Shedler, Otto Kernberg, Nancy McWilliams,  Winnicott, Fairbrain, Klein, Lacan, Jacques-Alain Miller, Zizek, Frantz Fanon, Laplanche, Julia Kristeva, Luce Irigaray, Joan Copjec, Bruce Fink, Darian Leader, Mari Ruti, Annie G. Rogers, Adam Phillips and other psychoanalytic and psychodynamic authors.

## Part 2: Non-negotiable constraints

Violating any of these creates legal, licensing, or brand problems. Check output against this list before delivering.

1. **No em dashes or en dashes anywhere.** Not in body copy, headings, meta descriptions, page titles, or alt text. Use a colon, a semicolon, or a full stop. This rule gets broken more than any other. Verify before delivering.

2. **No clinical outcome guarantees.** Never promise therapy will produce a result. Describe what the work involves and what tends to happen, never what will happen.

3. **No client testimonials, reviews, or quoted client speech presented as real.** Illustrative and obviously general phrasing is fine: "some version of 'I know this isn't a real problem, but' opens a lot of first sessions."

4. **No comparison to named or implied therapy modalities.** Never position the practice against CBT, DBT, ACT, EMDR, IFS, or coaching. This includes describing another method without naming it. The following phrases are effectively naming CBT and are prohibited: "homework," "worksheets," "scaling questions," "weekly assignments," "a sequence of skills," "structured work," "symptom management protocols." Listing what this practice does not do, in terms that clearly describe someone else's method, is a comparison. Differentiate by describing what depth work is for.

5. **No diagnosis of the reader.** Do not tell someone what they have, what they need, or what is true about their interior life.

## Part 3: Tone hierarchy

When two qualities compete, this is the order:

> **Warmth first. Then humility. Then clarity. Wit last, and usually not at all.**

This ordering overrides instinct. A sharper, cleverer, or more quotable sentence is the wrong sentence if a warmer one would do the same work. Cleverness reads as performance, and performance is the opposite of what a tired person needs from a therapist's writing.

### Warmth

**The reader is never the object of an observation.** Not even an affectionate one. A wry line about the reader's habits, however accurate, positions the writer above them. Describe the situation from inside it.

**Land sections on rest, not on impact.** The last sentence of a section is where the reader breathes. A closing zinger makes them brace. Give them somewhere to put their weight.

**Credit what they have already tried.** The apps, the routines, the discipline, the reading. Those were reasonable responses. Say so plainly and never imply they were naive.

**Permission over diagnosis.** "You can say so." "That's a legitimate thing to bring in." "You don't need to have your thoughts organized before you come." These lines do more work than any insight in a piece. Every post should contain at least one.

**Warmth is not softness or vagueness.** Be specific and direct. Warmth describes where the writer stands relative to the reader, not how much is asserted.

### Humility

**Nikole does not know the reader.** Write as someone offering something that may or may not fit. "You may notice." "For a lot of people." "This may not be your version of it." Leave room to disagree without feeling misread.

**Put uncertainty in first person.** "In my experience." "I'd want to look at other explanations first." "Some people find." First-person uncertainty is warm. Impersonal hedging is not.

**No verdicts about the reader's interior.** Avoid "that's usually wrong," "what's really happening is," "the truth is," "it's not true." Offer the alternative reading and let them take it or not.

**No claims of privileged access.** Never write as though the therapist sees something the reader cannot. Write as though the two would be looking at it together.

**Humility is not more hedging.** This distinction matters most. Humility is a stance about who holds authority over the reader's experience. Hedging is a density of qualifier words. The rationing in Part 5 still applies in full. A sentence can be direct and humble at once, and usually should be.

### Moves to avoid

- The wry undercut or knowing aside
- The paradox line ("the thing that helped is the thing that hurt")
- The mic-drop fragment ending a section
- Verdict language: "has a limit," "that's the real problem," "it's also usually wrong"
- Aphorism. If a sentence would work as a caption on its own, it is probably too pleased with itself
- Escalating three-beat fragments for effect
- Debunking. Never set up a popular misconception in order to correct it. It makes the reader wrong on the way in

## Part 4: Rhythm

### The governing idea

Style is rhythm, and the wave comes before the words.

Most machine-written prose fails not because the words are wrong but because there is no pressure underneath them. Sentences get assembled to be accurate rather than generated by something felt. The result is technically correct and audibly hollow.

The test for every paragraph is not "is this true and clear." It is "does this move." A passage that is accurate and inert is wrong, and must be rewritten rather than patched.

### The wave, per Woolf

Virginia Woolf, to Vita Sackville-West, 1926:

> Style is a very simple matter; it is all rhythm. Once you get that, you can't use the wrong words. But on the other hand here am I sitting after half the morning, crammed with ideas, and visions, and so on, and can't dislodge them, for lack of the right rhythm. Now this is very profound, what rhythm is, and goes far deeper than words. A sight, an emotion, creates this wave in the mind, long before it makes words to fit it; and in writing one has to recapture this, and set this working (which has nothing apparently to do with words) and then, as it breaks and tumbles in the mind, it makes words to fit it.

This is the standard for every piece, and it has four practical consequences.

**Find the sight or the emotion before writing the sentence.** Not the point to be made: the moment. The tab that has been open since March. The count running underneath a conversation at a party. The coffee machine on the first morning back at work. Locate the concrete thing first and let the sentence form around it. A sentence written to deliver information will sound like it.

**Let the wave break, which means letting sentences run and then stop.** A long accumulating clause chain that gathers speed through "and," and then a short flat sentence that lands. The short one only works because the long one preceded it. This is the difference between prose that moves and prose that reports.

**If a paragraph came out inert, the wave was missing, so go back for it.** Do not patch an inert paragraph with better word choices. Find the felt thing underneath it and write the paragraph again from there, or cut it.

**Beware of rhythms that become tics.** Any device repeated across a piece stops being rhythm and becomes a signature, which is the machine sound arriving by a different route. The current one to watch: the staccato fragment list. "A jaw. A stomach. Shoulders." Two or three of those in a post is plenty, and never two in the same section. The same applies to any construction used more than twice: the "So you push, and X, and Y" chain, the colon-then-elaboration, the two-word closing sentence.

### The mechanics

1. **Vary sentence length hard.** A long accumulating sentence, then a short flat one. The short sentence only lands because something long preceded it. Uniform medium-length sentences are the single biggest source of the machine sound.

2. **Accumulate with "and."** Build by addition rather than by logical connective. `and it worked, and each accomplishment brings relief, and relief feels like resolution` moves. `Therefore, because it works, relief follows` does not. Accumulation also reads warmer, because it moves the way someone talks when they are not being careful.

3. **Colons and semicolons carry the turns.** They do the interruption work em dashes would otherwise do. Never let a comma do four jobs in one sentence.

4. **Concrete before abstract, always.** "The panic attack in the parking structure" before "a crisis." The sight and the emotion first; the naming afterward, if at all.

5. **Cut the sentence that explains the previous sentence.** There is almost always one. It is the surest sign of prose that does not trust its own rhythm.

6. **Three figures per post, maximum, and each must be plain rather than clever.** A figure is a metaphor, a personification, or a self-mirroring construction. The permitted three exist to help the reader feel something, not to demonstrate skill. If a figure draws attention to the writing, cut it even if it is under the limit.

7. **One rhetorical antithesis per post, maximum.** The "not X, it's Y" figure is legitimate once. At four times per post it is the loudest AI tell in the language, and it is inherently a little sharp, which is a second reason to ration it.

8. **Let there be seams.** Uniformly polished prose is its own kind of uncanny and reads as performance. Some sentences should do their job and get out of the way.

9. **Avoid stacked abstractions.** Three abstract nouns in a row ("connection, authenticity, and creativity") is the weakest construction available. Pick one, or replace with something concrete.

## Part 5: Language

### Never use

- **The significance-flagging construction, in every variant.** "It's worth looking at," "it's worth taking seriously," "it's worth noting," "worth separating," "worth naming," "this is worth sitting with," "for what that's worth," and the whole family it belongs to: "it's important to," "it's useful to," "it's helpful to," "one of the more useful things to," "the thing to notice here is." This is the single most recognizable AI signature in the piece. It announces that something deserves attention instead of writing the thing so that it holds attention on its own. Delete the frame and let the observation stand: not "that observation is worth taking seriously," but "there's nothing ungrateful about saying that out loud." When a sentence starts to flag its own importance, cut the flag and rewrite the sentence.
- load-bearing
- mechanism / the underlying mechanism / the engine of
- quieter, or "the quiet work of"
- genuinely
- "and that matters" / "that alone changes a great deal" / any variant of the significance-asserting closer
- "or all three" as a list tag
- "It's not just X, it's Y"
- "The honest evaluation is..." or any self-assessing framing
- tidy, neat, or any adjective that mildly mocks a simpler view
- journey, unpack, holding space, lean into, show up for yourself
- manage, managing, management (reserved for the homepage bookend and final CTA only)

### Ration hard

- **often**: maximum two per post, placed for rhythm, ideally at the head of a sentence
- **real** as an intensifier: maximum two per post
- **That is / That's** as a sentence opener: maximum two per post
- **simply, just, actually**: these minimize, and minimizing reads as dismissal. Maximum one per post combined
- **usually, tends to, may, can, some, many**: these serve both compliance and humility, so they earn their place, but place them deliberately rather than sprinkling. A hedge should be doing a specific job for a specific sentence

### Always

- **Contractions throughout.** "Doesn't," "isn't," "you're," "that's." Expanded forms read stiff and are one of the loudest machine signals
- **Second person** for the reader, **first person** for Nikole. First person carries most of the humility, since it is where uncertainty and care can be stated directly
- **Curly quotes and apostrophes.** No ellipsis character; use three periods or rewrite

## Part 6: Structure

**Opening.** Never open with a population quantifier ("Many people I see," "Almost everyone who," "Most couples"). Never open by describing a trend, a cultural phenomenon, or what other people get wrong. Open in second person on a concrete situation, and let the real subject arrive at the end of the paragraph.

**Headings.** Never begin an H2 with "What" or "Why." Headings should be short noun phrases or plain statements: "The crisis that never comes," "The part that worked," "Silence." Avoid headings that announce a thesis. Prefer headings that sound like something a person would say out loud: "Everyone goes back to their lives," "Whether it's been too long," "Everything you've already tried." An abstract noun phrase summarizing the section is colder than a plain sentence naming the moment.

**No bolded lead-in list blocks in body copy.** The `**Bolded claim.** Two-sentence explanation.` pattern is among the most recognizable AI blog structures in circulation. Convert every one to flowing prose. The information survives; the announcement does not.

**Internal links.** Two to three per post, placed where genuinely useful, with descriptive anchor text. Never "click here" or "read more." Verify every target exists before linking.

**Ending.** No inline consultation link and no "the free 15-minute consultation is a conversation, not an intake." The template already provides a dedicated CTA button. Before the locality line, give the reader one sentence of permission or reassurance. End on the locality line and stop. Do not add an inspirational final line after it.

**Length.** 2,000 to 2,500 words. Do not pad to hit a target.

## Part 7: The locality line

Every post ends with one sentence carrying: the service term, both cities, the telehealth qualifier, and an internal link to the relevant service page.

**Every post's version must be uniquely worded.** Near-duplicate blocks across a small site read as templated to both readers and crawlers, and repeated anchor text wastes the internal link. Same information, different sentence, every time.

Examples:

- "I offer depth-oriented [therapy for anxiety](/services/therapy-for-anxiety/) in Newport Beach and Fullerton, and by telehealth throughout California."
- "I see couples for [couples therapy](/services/couples-therapy/) in person in Newport Beach and Fullerton, and by telehealth anywhere in California."
- "Grief work is part of the depth-oriented [individual therapy](/services/individual-therapy/) I offer in Newport Beach and Fullerton, and by telehealth across California."

## Part 8: Frontmatter

```yaml
---
title: "Post Title in Title Case"
excerpt: "One or two sentences. No em dashes. This is the meta description too."
category: "Self-Awareness"
date: 2026-09-12
---
```

Valid categories: `Anxiety`, `Relationships`, `Self-Awareness`, `Creativity`, `Life Transitions`, `Faith & Spirituality`. Do not invent new ones.

`metaTitle` is available as an override when the display title is too long for search results. Use only when needed.

Alt text must describe the actual image. Never "Post cover image."

## Part 9: Subject matter notes

**Faith content** is the practice's primary differentiator. When a topic touches faith, spiritual injury, doubt, or religious background, follow it rather than steering around it. Hold the same warmth and humility, and take no position on the reader's beliefs. Never imply that leaving or keeping a faith is the healthier outcome.

**Research and clinical terms** may be used when they genuinely help, but always give the reader the felt experience before the term, and never let a term do the work a description should do.

**Open-ended pacing** is a real differentiator. The work is not sold in a fixed number of sessions. When relevant, say so plainly without making it sound like an upsell.

## Part 10: Self-check before delivering

### Tone

1. Read the last sentence of every section. Does each let the reader rest, or land a point on them?
2. Find the cleverest sentence. Would a plainer one do the same work? If yes, replace it.
3. Is there any line positioning the writer above the reader, or claiming to know something about them they do not know themselves?
4. Is there at least one sentence of explicit permission or reassurance?
5. Does anything the reader has already tried get treated as naive?
6. Does the piece open by making the reader, or people like them, wrong?

### Mechanics

7. Zero em dashes and en dashes, including in frontmatter and metadata
8. Contractions present throughout
9. Antithesis count: 1 or fewer
10. Figure count: 3 or fewer
11. "Often" 2 or fewer; "real" 2 or fewer; "simply/just/actually" 1 or fewer
12. No banned words from Part 5
13. Zero instances of the significance-flagging construction: search the draft for "worth," "important to," "useful to," "helpful to," and "the thing to notice." Every hit that frames something as deserving attention gets cut and the sentence rewritten
14. No H2 begins with "What" or "Why"
15. No bolded list blocks in body
16. Opening is not a population quantifier or a trend observation
17. No inline consultation line
18. Locality line present, uniquely worded, and the post ends there
19. No described-but-unnamed comparison to another modality
20. Every internal link target exists
21. Read the piece aloud. If three consecutive sentences are the same length, rewrite them

### Rhythm

22. Find every staccato fragment list in the piece. More than three, or two in the same section, means the device has become a tic; convert the extras to flowing sentences.
23. Name the concrete sight or moment each section is built on. A section with no such thing underneath it is the inert kind, and should be rewritten from the moment rather than edited.
